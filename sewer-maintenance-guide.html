How Your Sewer Line Works

No pump, no motor, no electronics — your sewer line is applied physics. Understanding it is what separates a maintained line from a 2 AM backup.

01Gravity is the pump

Sewer lines slope downhill — about a quarter inch per foot — and gravity does all the work. Too little slope and solids settle; too much and water outruns them. Slope wrong, chronic trouble.

02The cleanout

A capped access pipe — usually outside near the house — that gives professionals direct entry to clear clogs and run cameras. Know where yours is before you need it at midnight.

03Municipal vs. septic

City sewer carries waste to treatment; septic treats it on-site in your yard. Most of Gwinnett County is municipal — but if you have a tank and drainfield, your maintenance rules are different.

04Vent stacks

Pipes through the roof let air into the system so drains flow smoothly and traps hold their water seal. Blocked vents cause the gurgling and slow drains homeowners blame on clogs.

05Traps

Every fixture’s U-bend holds water that blocks sewer gas from entering the home. Unused drains let their traps dry out — the simple science behind a mysterious bathroom odor.

06The lateral is yours

The line from your house to the street connection is typically the homeowner’s responsibility — including the part under the yard and sidewalk. Most owners learn this during their first backup.

Failure modes

What Causes Sewer Backups

  1. Tree roots.Roots detect moisture and infiltrate pipe joints and hairline cracks, then expand inside. Georgia’s clay soil and thirsty trees make this the region’s signature sewer problem — once roots are in, they catch everything flowing past.
  2. Grease — fats, oils, grease.Poured-down-the-drain grease solidifies in cool pipes, narrowing them layer by layer like arterial plaque. The most preventable cause of backups there is.
  3. Bellies and breaks.Sagging pipe sections (“bellies”) collect solids; cracked clay tile and shifted joints snag debris. A camera inspection is how professionals find what no plunger can fix.

Warning Signs Your Line Is in Trouble

Sewer lines rarely fail without announcing it first. These are the announcements.

01Multiple slow drains

One slow drain is a local clog. Several slow drains at once — sinks, tubs, toilets together — points to the main line, not the fixtures.

02Gurgling

Air trapped behind a developing blockage bubbles up through traps. If drains talk, listen.

03Sewage odor indoors

Persistent odor suggests dry traps, vent problems, or worse. Occasional whiffs from an unused drain are normal; a constant smell deserves investigation.

04Water where it shouldn’t be

Flushing the toilet sends water up into the shower or tub — the textbook main-line backup symptom. Stop using water and call a professional.

05Suspiciously lush grass

An unusually green strip over the sewer line can mean a leak is fertilizing — and feeding the roots that will widen it.

06Backups after rain

Stormwater infiltrating cracked lines can overwhelm capacity. If trouble always follows heavy rain, the line itself — not your habits — is suspect.

Hazards of Sewer Backups

A sewer backup is not just unpleasant. It is a health hazard and a property disaster in one event.

01Health hazard

Sewage may carry harmful bacteria and pathogens. Avoid contact with backup water, keep children and pets away, and don’t attempt cleanup of significant backups yourself — this is professional work with proper protection.

02Property damage

Backups ruin flooring, drywall, and belongings — and porous materials exposed to sewage generally must be removed, not cleaned. The longer water sits, the wider the damage.

03Mold follows

Everything a backup soaks starts the 24–48 hour mold clock. Sewage cleanup and structural drying have to happen together, fast.

04Repeat events

An uncleared root intrusion or an unrepaired belly guarantees encores. Clearing the symptom without fixing the line is a subscription to future backups.

05Beyond your property line

A neglected lateral can create problems for neighbors and municipal systems. Sewer maintenance is one of the few home chores with community consequences.

Maintenance That Prevents Backups

  • Know where your sewer cleanout is?
  • No grease down drains — and no wipes flushed, “flushable” or not?
  • Trees planted well away from the sewer line’s path?
Straight answers

Sewer Science FAQs

Straight answers to the questions homeowners ask most about their sewer lines.

Are “flushable” wipes really flushable?

No. They don’t break down like toilet paper and are a leading cause of household clogs and municipal blockages. The label describes the trip down the toilet — not what happens in your pipes afterward. Trash them instead.

What is a sewer cleanout?

A capped access pipe — usually outside near the house — that gives professionals direct entry into the sewer line for clearing clogs and running inspection cameras. If you don’t know where yours is, find it before an emergency.

How do tree roots get into sewer pipes?

Through pipe joints, hairline cracks, and loose connections — roots detect moisture and grow toward it, then expand inside. Older clay tile and cast-iron lines with many joints are the most vulnerable; Georgia’s clay soil and thirsty trees make root intrusion the region’s signature sewer problem.

What is a backwater valve?

A device a plumber installs on the sewer line that lets wastewater flow out but automatically closes to prevent municipal sewer backups from flowing back into your home. It’s the standard protection for homes in areas prone to system overloads during heavy rain.
