Common Causes of Water Damage in Buford & Gwinnett County

Buford's water risk follows its geography: Lake Lanier's shoreline and high water table on one side, decades-old mill-town housing near downtown on the other, and fast-growing subdivisions, plus the commercial boom around the Mall of Georgia, changing how water moves across the land in between. These are the incidents local restoration contractors see most often in and around Buford.

01Lake Lanier shoreline moisture

Buford's Lake Lanier shoreline homes live with a high water table and constant lakeside humidity. Basements and crawlspaces near the shore take on hydrostatic pressure after rain, and the ambient dampness means any leak turns into a mold problem faster than it would inland.

02Commercial corridor runoff

The Mall of Georgia area's vast parking lots and rooftops turn rainfall into runoff, and ongoing development around the corridor keeps redrawing local drainage patterns. Neighborhoods downhill of new construction can see more street and yard flooding than they did five years ago, not because it rains more, but because the water has fewer places to go.

03Historic mill-town housing

Downtown Buford grew up around the mills, and its older housing stock carries aging plumbing, old roofs, and decades of patchwork repairs. Burst pipes and slow leaks behind walls are the signature water events in these homes.

04New subdivisions, builder-grade fixtures

Buford's growth corridors are full of newer construction — where builder-grade supply valves, toilet connectors, and washing-machine hoses are the weak points. They fail early and without warning, often while nobody is home.

05Lake tributaries & summer storms

Fast, heavy rain overwhelms gutters and grading in a single afternoon, and the low-lying streets near Lake Lanier's tributary creeks pond deep enough to push water into garages. On the older mill-town housing stock near downtown, wind-driven rain exploits aging shingles and decades of patchwork repairs.

06Sewer backups & root intrusion

Buford's mix of old and new development means a mix of old and new sewer lines — and the old ones run through mature tree cover. Root intrusion plus heavy rain inflow produces the sewage backups that surface through floor drains and lower-level fixtures.

From Water Extraction to Mold Remediation: The 5-Step Restoration Process

Every property is different, but professional water damage restoration follows the same sequence. These steps are performed by the independent contractor your call is routed to.

01Inspection & assessment

The contractor finds the water source, classifies the water as clean, gray or black, and maps how far moisture has traveled using meters and thermal imaging.

02Water extraction

Standing water comes out first with pumps and high-powered extractors. Fast extraction is what keeps a bad day from becoming a total loss.

03Drying & dehumidification

Air movers and commercial dehumidifiers run for days while technicians track moisture readings. In Georgia humidity, this step decides whether mold takes hold.

04Cleaning & sanitizing

Affected surfaces get antimicrobial treatment and salvageable contents are cleaned. Sewage and gray-water jobs follow a stricter decontamination protocol.

05Mold remediation & repairs

Compromised drywall, insulation and flooring are removed, remaining areas are treated, and the space is rebuilt — so the property comes back whole, not just dry.

Water Damage Restoration FAQs: Buford, Georgia

Common questions from homeowners in Buford and Gwinnett County — answered plainly.

My home is near Lake Lanier. Does that change my water damage risk?

Yes, in two ways: the high water table puts hydrostatic pressure on basements and crawlspaces after rain, and the constant lakeside humidity means leaks stay wet longer — giving mold a head start. If you have a basement or crawlspace near the shore, a sump pump with battery backup and a dehumidifier are worth every penny. And check whether you're in a flood zone; lake-adjacent properties often are.

Do I really need a sump pump with battery backup?

If your home has a below-grade space and you've ever seen water in it, yes. Pumps fail most often during the storms that cause the flooding — when the power goes out. A battery backup keeps the pump running through an outage, which is exactly when you need it. Test the whole setup twice a year; a pump that hasn't run in two years may not run when it matters.

A pipe burst while we were away for the weekend. What now?

Shut off the main water valve first — know where it is before you need it. Document everything with photos before cleanup begins, and call your insurer early. Then get a restoration contractor out fast: water that has sat for days has soaked deep into subflooring and wall cavities, and in lake-area humidity, mold is already on the clock. Don't run your HVAC until the affected areas are assessed — it can spread contamination and moisture.
